The open benchmark for LLM gateways

Runix Bench measures any OpenAI-compatible gateway or relay from the outside, the way your application sees it: price against the official list price, time to first token and throughput, success rate over 30 days, and whether the model behind the endpoint is the one on the label. Every raw result is public.

Open source under the MIT licence. Measure, don't trust: the probe, the prompts and the data are in the repository, so a result can be checked rather than believed.

03 · Capabilities

Five questions, in the order they matter

A team picking a gateway asks whether it can trust it, then whether it is cheap, then whether it stays up. The benchmark answers in that order, and reads a low price next to trust: a gateway far under list price is a warning sign, not a bargain.

01

Trust and integrity

Is it the real model, and will the operator still be there? A fixed request set goes to the gateway and to the official API at once, following the K2 Vendor Verifier method, and the answers are compared. Data retention and the legal entity are annotated with links to the source.

02

Price against list

The gateway's price divided by the official list price, per model and as one index across models. Cache pricing and hidden multipliers are recorded beside it.

03

Stability over 30 days

Success rate from a probe every six hours, with rate limits, outages and timeouts counted apart. Errors the probe causes itself, such as an unauthorised key, are excluded and shown separately.

04

Speed

Time to first token at p50, and p95 only when a run has at least five samples. Throughput is counted after the first token, the convention llmperf and Artificial Analysis use. Fake streaming, a reply buffered and then dumped, is flagged.

05

Cache

Whether a repeated prompt is billed and served as a cache hit, measured rather than taken from the price page.

06

Three ratings, no fourth

Each claim is verified, claimed but unverified, or unverified. Nothing without an evidence link is ever marked verified, and a related party is never rated a tier higher for the relationship.

04 · How it works

Where it sits, and how it measures

The benchmark stands where your application stands: outside the gateway, calling its public endpoint. It needs no access to anyone's infrastructure and no cooperation from the operator.

01

A public probe

The probe script and its prompts are in the repository. Each request carries a random id so a gateway cannot serve it from a cache, output is capped, and concurrency stays at four or under so a run never becomes a load test.

02

Scheduled, then committed

A scheduled job probes every tracked endpoint every six hours and commits the raw result. Leaderboard figures come from a rolling 30-day window; older runs stay in the data and do not move the table.

03

Agent runs on Terminal-Bench

Beyond single calls, the same Terminal-Bench tasks can run through two routers with Claude Code or Codex as the agent, verifier scores and costs side by side, with A/A and A/B statistics. These runs are hosted by our team on request.

04

Same models, same prompts

Every endpoint is probed on the same tracked models, each under the name that gateway gives it, so a price or speed figure compares one model across gateways rather than whatever each one serves by default.

05

Evidence for what is annotated

What a probe cannot measure, a data-retention policy or the legal entity behind a service, is annotated by hand with a link to the source and the date it was read. Corrections are taken as pull requests.

06

Unknown stays unknown

A measurement that could not be taken is shown as missing, never as zero. Network latency from a scheduled runner is labelled as an estimate, and the probe region is printed on every figure.

05 · Why open

Why a gateway company publishes a gateway benchmark

We sell a gateway, so a benchmark we run could be read as marketing. It is built so that it does not have to be trusted: the method is written down before the numbers, the raw data is public, and anyone can rerun it against the same endpoints with their own key. That is the same rule we hold our own products to.

09 · Questions

Common questions

What does Runix Bench measure?

Five things about an LLM gateway or relay, in the order they matter to the team that depends on it: whether it can be trusted (the model behind the endpoint, data retention, a legal entity), its price against the official list price, its success rate over 30 days, its speed (time to first token and throughput), and whether repeated prompts hit a cache.

Is it free?

Yes. The probe, the methodology and every raw result are open source under the MIT licence, and the self-test runs on your own machine with your own key. Managed probing and report hosting are described on the benchmark site.

Does my API key leave my machine?

No. The self-test runs locally and calls the gateway you point it at directly. The key is read from an environment variable and is not written to the report.

Is Runix Router in the table?

Not today. One gateway in the table is run by a related party; it is marked as such on its row and rated by the same rules as everyone else, never a tier higher for the relationship.

How often is the table updated?

Every six hours, from a scheduled job that commits each run to the repository. Success rates are computed over a rolling 30-day window, with the 7-day figure beside it.

Why would a gateway company publish a gateway benchmark?

Because a buyer should not have to trust any gateway's claims, including ours. The benchmark is built to be checked rather than believed: the prompts are in the script, the raw data is public and anyone can rerun it against the same endpoints.
